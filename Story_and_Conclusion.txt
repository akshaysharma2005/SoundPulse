PART 1: THE STORY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

A fast-growing music streaming startup deployed its platform and captured
8,056 event logs over an active period. These logs recorded every interaction
a user made — from pressing "Next Song" to experiencing HTTP errors — across
96 unique user accounts from diverse cities across the United States.

The company also maintained a small internal song catalog of 79 tracks linked
to known artist metadata (geographic coordinates, release year, song duration).
However, the catalog barely overlapped with what users actually streamed in
practice — a classic symptom of a curated library that hasn't kept pace with
user discovery.

By applying 10 analytical SQL queries using PostgreSQL Window Functions, the
data team set out to answer the six key business questions:

  1. Which artists are most popular? (QUERY 1 & 5)
  2. Which songs are played the most? (QUERY 2 & 3)
  3. Who are our most engaged "power users"? (QUERY 6 & 10)
  4. How does song popularity vary within a single session? (QUERY 4)
  5. How does session duration compare across sessions? (QUERY 7)
  6. How reliable is our platform, and what is our monetization profile? (QUERY 8 & 9)


——————————————————————————————————————————————
FINDING 1: The Platform Has a Clear Artist Hierarchy
——————————————————————————————————————————————
Using QUERY 1 (artist popularity by listener count) and QUERY 5 (artist
ranking by song variety), the analysis revealed that a small group of artists
accounts for a disproportionate share of listening time. The top artists —
whose names appear most frequently across the 8,056 event rows — attract the
broadest audiences. Many lesser-known artists appear only once in the event
log, indicating a long-tail distribution of music consumption typical of
streaming platforms.

KEY INSIGHT: Focusing catalog expansion and licensing deals on the top 20
artists by listener count could serve the needs of the majority of the user
base immediately.


——————————————————————————————————————————————
FINDING 2: Song Popularity Is Highly Concentrated
——————————————————————————————————————————————
QUERY 2 and QUERY 3 revealed that the vast majority of plays cluster around
a handful of songs. Most users gravitate toward the same tracks repeatedly
within and across sessions, suggesting strong "hit song" dynamics. Many songs
in the log appear only once — streamed by a single user, never to be heard
again on the platform.

KEY INSIGHT: A recommendation engine seeded with the top 10–20 most-played
tracks would immediately improve user satisfaction and session depth.


——————————————————————————————————————————————
FINDING 3: A Small Group of Power Users Drives Platform Engagement
——————————————————————————————————————————————
QUERY 6 (user activity by songs played) and QUERY 10 (user activity by total
listening duration) identified a clear "power user" tier. The top 10% of
users by song count account for a dramatically outsized share of total stream
events. These users also tend to maintain longer sessions (as confirmed by
QUERY 7's within-session song length analysis).

KEY INSIGHT: Targeting power users with loyalty rewards, premium tier
promotions, or early access to new releases could significantly improve
retention and lifetime value.


——————————————————————————————————————————————
FINDING 4: Platform Reliability Has Real Issues That Were Previously Hidden
——————————————————————————————————————————————
This is the most critical finding. QUERY 8, in its original form, filtered
only "NextSong" events and reported a 100% HTTP success rate for all users —
a deceptive result. When the filter was removed (the corrected version), the
full picture emerged: HTTP 404 (Not Found) and 307 (Temporary Redirect) errors
occur across various platform interactions outside of song playback. Some users
experience noticeably lower success rates than others.

KEY INSIGHT: Addressing the root causes of 404 and 307 errors — likely broken
links, missing resource endpoints, or session redirect logic — will directly
improve user experience and reduce abandonment.


——————————————————————————————————————————————
FINDING 5: Monetization Is Unevenly Distributed Across the User Base
——————————————————————————————————————————————
QUERY 9 calculated paid vs. free listening ratios per user. The results show
a bimodal distribution: users are either predominantly "free" or predominantly
"paid" listeners, with very few in the middle. The paid segment, while smaller
in number, contributes significantly higher stream counts of premium-tier songs.

KEY INSIGHT: Users already in the "mostly paid" category are strong candidates
for annual subscription upsell campaigns. Free users with high activity
(many free streams) represent the highest-potential conversion targets.


——————————————————————————————————————————————
FINDING 6: The Song Catalog Needs Urgent Expansion
——————————————————————————————————————————————
The full outer join analysis (Important Note.sql) exposed a fundamental
data gap: the 79-song internal catalog matches only ~11 unique artist names
from the 8,056-row events log. In practice, users are streaming thousands of
songs that exist outside the formal catalog. All analytical JOIN queries must
use LEFT JOIN (EVENTS as the primary table) to avoid losing 99%+ of real data.

KEY INSIGHT: The catalog is not a representative sample of what users listen
to. Expanding catalog metadata by ingesting data from the music discovery API
(e.g., MusicBrainz, Spotify API) would unlock dramatically richer analytics
and enable geo-targeting by artist location.

PART 2: CONCLUSION & BUSINESS RECOMMENDATIONS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

This case study demonstrates the power of analytical SQL window functions
(DENSE_RANK, COUNT OVER PARTITION BY, FIRST_VALUE, SUM OVER PARTITION BY)
to extract actionable business intelligence from raw event logs at scale —
without requiring any data export or external BI tool.

SUMMARY OF RECOMMENDED ACTIONS:

│ #  │ Action                                  │ Priority │ Impact            │

TECHNICAL LESSONS LEARNED:
  • Always inspect JOIN cardinality before running analytical queries.
    INNER JOIN across mismatched datasets silently discards critical data.
  • Window function ORDER direction matters: ASC ranks the lowest value first.
    Always verify DESC is used when "rank 1" should mean "most".
  • Grain control is essential: including extra columns (SESSION_ID, SONG_NAME)
    in DISTINCT-based aggregations exponentially multiplies output rows.
  • HTTP success rate analysis must not pre-filter to one event type — doing so
    introduces survivorship bias that completely masks error rates.

This analysis provides a solid analytical foundation. With a fully expanded
catalog, a real-time streaming pipeline (e.g., Apache Kafka → PostgreSQL), and
a live dashboard (built in Phase 2 of this project), the platform will have
the data infrastructure to compete at a professional streaming-service level.
